## Comment on Material Classification 
- In Materials Engineering, we tend to group materials into one of the following four material categories: 
	- metals
	- ceramics 
	- polymers 
	- composites 
- These groups are formed with common material properties, type of elements, application and fabrication techniques

## Definition: Ceramics and Glass
- Ceramics: name comes from Greek "keramikos", which means 'burnt stuff'.
	- Composition - compounds of metallic and nonmetallic elements (sometimes between two non metallic elements).
	- Fabrication - forming particles of the material into the desired shape, and then firing the material at high temperature to attach the particles together. 
	- Types of bonds - interatomic onds that form can range from totally or predominantly ionic, to entirely covalent. They can have limited or a great deal of crystalline structure with long range order. 
- Glasses have the same constituent and local bonds as do ceramics, but are non-crystalline (amorphous) and have no long range order 
	- Fabrication - formed by heating particles of material to liquid form, and cooling fast enough to allowing them to form bonds without crystallization.

## Advantages of ceramics 
- Properties can be tuned to perform under extreme conditions
- Advantages may include (depending on final application)
	- High melting point (excellent thermal insulator)
	- High creep resistance 
	- High elastic modulus 
	- High hardness 
	- High wear resistance 
	- Excellent compressive strength 
	- Chemically inactive (often resistant to most acids, alkalis and organic solvents)
	- High electrical resistivity 
- Disadvantages may include 
	- Low density 
	- Low ductility (very brittle)
	- Low fracture toughness 
	- Potentially difficult to manufacture.

## The high strength values of ceramics is due to restricted dislocation motion (slip) relative to other materials 
- In metals, dislocation motion is inherently easy because all atoms are electrically neutral, and electrons can move freely. 
- Metals can be strengthened by work hardening (plastic deformation introduces dislocations which makes future deformations more difficult)
- With covalently-bonded ceramics, dislocation motion is difficult because strong covalent bonds have to be broken and reformed 
	- Work hardening cannot occur  (no plastic deformation)
- Ionically-bonded ceramics, dislocation motion (slip) is restricted by the distribution of charges within the crystal structure 
	- Work hardening cannot occur

## Fracture Strength of Ceramics - Tension vs. Compression, and a large variation in failure strengths due to flaws.
- Same fracture toughness $K_{IC}$ as with metals. 
- $K_{IC}$ is typically $0.5MPa \sqrt{ m }$ for glasses (metals can be 50- 100x larger)
- $K_{IC} = Y\sigma_{c}\sqrt{ \pi a_{c} }$
- Fracture strength is related to the presence of flaws in the specimen, which causes stress concentration points 
- In ceramic materials, the size and quantity of flaws are not as well characterized or controlled as in metals.
- Since these flaws can vary greatly from sample to sample, they result in a wide variation in the measured fracture strength values. 
- Compressive strength values can be 10-15 times higher than the tensile strength, since flaws do not result in stress amplification during compression

## Ceramics Fabrication 
- Ceramics are very fragile and brittle, and therefore difficult to machine into a desired shape 
- Instead the process typically starts with a powdered form of ceramic, which can be formed into the desired shape before heat treatment to finalize the part. 
- The ceramics fabrication process involves 4 major stages: 

- Powder
	- Mill (grind) and screen constituents to obtain desired particle size 
	- Minerals and materials like clay, silica, feldspar and others are extracted from the earth and ground to powder form. 
- Forming
	- Mix the powder with water or keep dry.
	- Press or cast the powder into the desired shape. The resulting part is still soft and pliable. 
- Drying 
	- Drive out excess water before firing.
- Firing
	- Firing at a high temperature causes either vitrification or sintering. 

## 2. Forming - 3 ways of taking ceramic powder into a desired shape. 
- Powder pressing can be used for both clay and non-clay and non-clay compositions (such as metals). This is a dry process, no water is added. 
- Hydroplastic forming involves extruding a thick mix of water + ceramic powder mass through an die with a desired cross section.
- Slip Casting - "slip" is a fluid containing a mix of water and ceramic powder. Pour the slip into a water-absorbing mold, wait for water absorb, then pour out the remaining slip. The longer the waiting time before pouring, the thicker the shell. 

## 3. Drying and 4. Firing
- Drying - as water is removed, the inter-particle spacing decrease, resulting in overall shrinkage. Drying too fast causes sample to warp or crack due to non-uniform shrinkage 
- Firing - heat treatment between 900-1400, results in one of two outcomes: 
a) Sintering 
	- occurs when firing a piece below the melting temperature (no liquid formed) and where the piece has been powder pressed (no added water). The particles coalesce due to an overall reduction in surface area due to surface energy. Gaps between particles are also reduced. 
b) Vitrification 
- liquid glass (amorphous solid) forms from clay and flux which can flow between $SiO_{2}$ particles (flix are materials which lower melting temperature). Makes it strong and waterproof 
- Fired porcelain sample (melting/vitrification temp 1330 degC)

## Network Modifiers in Silica Glass - Reduce Viscosity, Lower Melting Temperatures. 
- Network Modifiers are additives which break up the short-range order bonds 
- $Na_{2}O$ reduces the glass viscosity and melting point allowing the glass to be formed at lower temperature 
- $CaO$ - strengthens the glass structure, improves durability, prevents solubility in water 
- $Al_{2}O_{3}$ - increases hardness and scratch resistance 
- $K_{2}O$ - larger potassium ions replace smaller sodium ions, creating compressive stresses. 
- Soda-lime glass makes up 90% of all commercial glasses 
- Soda $Al_{2}O_{3}$ +$K_{2}O$ are key components in Gorilla Glass for you phones.

## Solidification of Silica and Glass Silica
- $T_{g}=$ glass-transition temperature 
- $T_{m}=$ melting temperature of the crystalline solid. 
- When silica is cooled from the liquid state, the glass will not normally 'crystallize' at Tm;
- The formation of distinct crystallographic structures involving $SiO_{4}$ tetrahedra is too difficult: 
	- Extremely slow cooling is employed 
	- nucleating agents are added to assist in forming the initial crystallization sites
- Therefore, silica can exist as a supercooled liquid below $T_{m}$

## Solidification of Silica and Glass Silica 
- For cooling of a supercooled liquid, a change in slope occurs at $T_{g}$.
- Above $T_{g}$, it is considered a supercooled liquid (cooled below its conventional melting point $T_{m}$)
- Note that during heating, if the material is in crystalline solid form, it does not start to melt or form any liquid until reaches the Tm melting temperature. 
- The concept of glass transition temperature and crystallization are particularly useful when dealing with polymers and plastics, where $T_{m}$ and $T_{G}$ are generally closer to ambient temperatures. 

![[Pasted image 20251210212503.png]]
- Heat treating glass to reduce cracking and failure - Annealing vs Tempering 
	- Annealing - elevate temperature to remove thermal stresses resulting from inhomogeneous temperatures during cooling 
	- Most window glass is annealed 
		- Glass that has not been properly annealed retains thermal stresses which indefinitely decreases the strength and reliability 
- Tempering - Heat glass above glass transition temperature $T_{g}$ but below softening point and then quench in an air jet or oil bath. 
- The interior cools after the exterior, and tries to contract after exterior has already become rigid. This causes residual compressive stresses on surface and tensile stresses inside. Forming tempered glass.
- Combination of residual stresses and high fracture stress means high storage of elastic energy. 
- Release of elastic energy at fracture causes glass to break up into smaller granular pieces instead of jagged shards. 

## Glass Tempering 
### 3 step heat treatments 
1. Heat to temperature just below softening point
2. Quench the surface in an inert gas, surface cools while interior stays hot 
3. Allow center to cool to room temperature.

Resulting stress distribution has the outer surfaces with a final compressive stress, while interior has a tensile stress.
