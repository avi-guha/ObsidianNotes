
## Heat Treatment 
### Why Heat Treat
- Produce specific properties, such as yield strength, ductility, and toughness, achieved through manipulation of microstructure 
- To relieve stresses caused by dislocations and grain boundaries 
- To use non-equilibrium heating and cooling to create microstructures which do not appear on phase diagram, which have desirable properties. 

### Why we need to know this 
- Mechanical properties of an alloy that has been heat treated can be altered if it is re-heated. 

### Same Temp profile can be used in different materials to increase strength and reduce ductility due to different mechanisms. 
- Annealing (raising temp to convert sample into one uniform phase reducing effect of cold work, increase grain size, reduce internal stress). Normalizing or solutionizing (get rid of defects.)

### Form 1: Bainite - same composition as pearlite but in needle-like form 
- Remember carbon must diffuse in order to form distinct pearlite layers 
- If the sample is quenched to very low temp, less time for diffusion to occur 
- Instead of layers/lamellae, new structures which look like thin needles will form. This is Bainite.
	- Carbon diffuses to form distinct pearlite layers
	- Instead of lamellae, new structures with thin needles of Fe3C in a ferrite matrix form bainite
- ![[Pasted image 20251210164345.png]]


## Form 2: Martensite - Harder and More brittle 
- If temp is cooled fast and low enough, there is no time for diffusion. Ferrite and cementite do not have time to form (no pearlite)
- New supersaturated and unstable structure (martensite) is formed,
- The lattice is stuck between a FCC and BCC. WE get a body centered tetragonal (BCT).
- Some austenite may stay locked in place as Austenite. 
- Martensite is relatively common.
- ![[Pasted image 20251210164616.png]]

## Characteristics of Martensite Transformation 
- Occurs by non-diffusional process - very fast 
- Transformation occurs at temperature well below eutectoid temp. 
- Martensitic / diffusion-less transformations can occur in other metals 
- Martensite in steel is very hard high ($\sigma_{y}$) and extremely brittle. Not useful for engineering 
	- Can be tempered to get a useful steel material 
	- Some modern applications use un-tempered martensite.

## Heat Treatment after Quenching: Tempering Martensite 
- Tempering involves reheating to moderate temperature after quenching to regain ductility and toughness.
- Heat martensite for 1 hour at 200-550.
	- BCT (alpha') phase transfroems to BBC (alpha) 
	- very fine distribution of precipitates is formed into alpha Matrix
	- Creates a structure with reduced hardness but greater ductility 
	- We can accurately control the desired amount of change in hardness and ductility because there is good control over temp and time. 
- If we hold at temp for too long, steel becomes too soft.

### Annealing, Normalizing, Tempering - all heat treatments where the temperature is raised and held for some time to generate microstructure changes 
- Not well defined
	- Annealing - used to reduce internal stresses (make as soft as possible) minimal imperfections
	- Normalizing - similar to annealing, faster cooling, more imperfection, slightly harder, less ductile 
	- Tempering - refers to process of reducing hardness and increasing toughness of iron-based alloys 
	- Solutionizing refers to heating up the sample to create only a single phase of solid material. 

## Spheroidite 
- Formed from tempering pearlite 
	- Start with P, raise to high temp for long time. 
		- cementite layers evolve to dispersion of spheres 
		- This reduces the phase boundary area, increases ductility, decreases yield strength 
	- Similar to tempered martensite, but much larger spheres.

# Mechanism B - Age Hardening 
- Mahir strengthening mechanism for alloys of Al, Fe, Cu and some steels. 
- Strength and hardness can peak and start to diminish if we age for too long. 

### Age Hardening Temp Profile 
1. Heat treatment 
	- Form a single phase solid solution 
2. Quench 
	- Enter a 2 phase region of phase diagram but does not form hardened martensitic type microstructure
3. Age 
	- Elevate the temperture, and generate a distribution of very small precipitates, which increase hardness and decrease ductility. 
- The profile is identical to Quench-Temper, but very different mechanism and produces opposite result. 

## Required conditions for Age Hardening 
- Age hardening occurs only in alloys that: 
	- show appreciable solid solubility of one alloying component in the other. 
	- Show a solubility limit that rapidly decreases in concentration of one major component with temp reduction.![[Pasted image 20251210170626.png]]


## Age Hardening - Microstructural Changes
