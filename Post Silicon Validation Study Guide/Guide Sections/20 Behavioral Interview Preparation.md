## What Tesla-style technical interviews may value

- Ownership.
- Technical depth.
- Speed with discipline.
- First-principles reasoning.
- Comfort with ambiguity.
- Hands-on execution.
- Evidence-based decisions.
- Clear communication.
- Learning from failure.
- High standards.

## STAR structure

### Situation

Provide brief context.

### Task

Explain your responsibility.

### Action

Describe what you personally did.

### Result

State the measurable outcome and lesson.

## Strong debugging-story structure

1. Describe the system.
2. Describe the failure.
3. Explain why it was difficult.
4. List the hypotheses.
5. Explain the measurements.
6. Show how you narrowed the issue.
7. State the root cause.
8. Explain the fix.
9. Explain how you verified it.
10. State what you would improve.

## Behavioral questions

### Tell me about a difficult hardware bug.

Focus on:

- Initial uncertainty.
- Structured isolation.
- Instrumentation.
- Evidence.
- Final resolution.

### Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate.

Show:

- Respect.
- Technical evidence.
- Listening.
- Experimental resolution.
- Focus on the project rather than ego.

### Tell me about a failure.

Show:

- Ownership.
- What you missed.
- What you changed.
- How you prevented recurrence.

### Tell me about a time you learned something quickly.

Show:

- How you identified the learning gap.
- How you built a minimal test.
- How you validated your understanding.
- How you applied it.

### Tell me about working under time pressure.

Show:

- Prioritization.
- Risk management.
- Communication.
- Testing the highest-risk assumptions first.

## What each behavioral signal means

- **Ownership:** You took responsibility for clarifying the problem, driving next steps, and following through, even if the bug crossed team boundaries.
- **Technical depth:** You can explain not only what happened, but why it happened at the hardware/software level.
- **Speed with discipline:** You moved quickly while still preserving evidence, checking assumptions, and avoiding reckless changes.
- **First-principles reasoning:** You worked from power, clocks, reset, data flow, timing, and specifications instead of only relying on guesses or pattern matching.
- **Comfort with ambiguity:** You could make progress before all facts were known by forming hypotheses and designing experiments.
- **Evidence-based decisions:** You used measurements, logs, counters, waveforms, or controlled comparisons to choose the next step.

## What makes a strong story

A strong story includes the system context, your personal responsibility, the exact failure, why it was hard, the evidence you collected, the decision points, the root cause, the fix, and the regression or process improvement that followed.

Avoid making the story sound like luck. Interviewers are listening for method. Even if the final bug was simple, the value is in how you narrowed the search space and proved the answer.
